Abstract—Commercial airlines rely on automated dynamic revenue management systems to adjust ticket prices based on booking horizon, unallocated inventory depletion, and passenger price elasticity. This pricing volatility creates profound information asymmetry for travelers seeking to minimize travel expenditures. This research paper presents an end-to-end unsupervised machine learning framework that segments commercial airline routes according to historical pricing dynamics, fare volatility, stage length, and departure-week surge intensity. Leveraging 70,000 flight price observations expanded across 112 commercial corridors from the Kaggle Flight Price Prediction dataset, we engineer 9 corridor-level behavioral dimensions. We benchmark standard Euclidean distance K-Means against Manhattan distance formulations to evaluate performance on positively skewed airfare distributions. Hyperparameter sweeps across cluster sizes from K equals 2 to 8 demonstrate that K equals 4 represents the optimal structural segmentation, confirmed by an inflection in the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS = 241.66) and a peak Silhouette score of 0.4858 under the Manhattan metric. Principal Component Analysis verifies that 3 orthogonal components capture 88.6% of total cumulative variance. Cluster partition stability tests across 10 random seeds yield an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.9927, proving 99.3% deterministic stability. The four discovered archetypes are operationalized into an interactive decision engine that identifies the optimal booking window between 18 and 34 days prior to departure, protecting consumers from surge escalations of up to 44%.
Keywords—Airfare Segmentation, Unsupervised Clustering, K-Means, Manhattan Distance, Revenue Management, Dynamic Pricing, Principal Component Analysis, Yield Intelligence, Partition Stability.

I. Introduction

Commercial aviation is an economically vital industry characterized by high fixed capital costs, perishable seat inventory, volatile jet fuel expenditures, and fiercely competitive market structures. Since the global deregulation of commercial air transportation in the late 1970s, passenger airlines have abandoned static distance-based tariff schedules in favor of sophisticated automated Revenue Management Systems (RMS). Modern airline pricing engines aim to maximize Revenue per Available Seat-Kilometer (RASK) by dynamically partitioning passenger demand into micro-market willingness-to-pay tiers.

Under contemporary yield management regimes, the fare charged for an identical seat on a specific flight fluctuates dramatically over time. Ticket prices are continually adjusted in response to booking lead times, remaining unallocated seat inventory, competitor flight offerings, day-of-week travel patterns, and macroeconomic demand shocks. For business travelers with inelastic demand and short planning horizons, airlines aggressively escalate prices as departure dates approach. Conversely, for price-sensitive leisure travelers with high demand elasticity, carriers release discounted early-bird inventory buckets weeks or months in advance.

While dynamic pricing algorithms maximize carrier load factors and profitability, they create substantial search friction, financial anxiety, and information asymmetry for consumers. Individual travelers encounter the fundamental booking optimization dilemma: "Should tickets be purchased immediately, or will fares decline closer to the departure date?" In the absence of transparent historical pricing benchmarks, consumers frequently succumb to departure-week price gouging or purchase prematurely at suboptimal advance rates.

Traditional econometric literature has approached airfare modeling predominantly through supervised regression paradigms, such as Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), Box-Cox transformations, and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) time-series forecasting. While regression models are capable of estimating point-in-time fare values for specific flights, they treat individual flight tickets as disconnected, atomized transactions. Consequently, supervised approaches fail to capture macro-level corridor structural behavior. Different flight routes exhibit fundamentally distinct revenue strategies: a dense regional corporate shuttle experiences severe departure-week price spikes, whereas an ultra-long-haul intercontinental route exhibits flatter, advance-purchase booking horizons.

To overcome these limitations, this research introduces an unsupervised machine learning architecture designed to discover latent pricing archetypes across 112 commercial airline corridors. By abstracting raw flight records into corridor-level behavioral vectors, we investigate whether commercial flight routes naturally cluster into defensible pricing profiles that reflect underlying airline revenue management strategies.

The primary contributions of this paper are organized as follows:

1) Corridor-Level Feature Engineering: We transform 70,000+ individual flight observations into 112 Origin-Destination (O&D) corridor vectors across 9 behavioral pricing dimensions, capturing fare scale, volatility, surge velocity, and prime corporate slot markups.

2) Mathematical Distance Metric Benchmark: We systematically benchmark Euclidean (L2) distance against Manhattan (L1) distance, providing analytical and empirical justification for the superiority of Manhattan distance when clustering heavy-tailed, positively skewed financial data.

3) Quantitative Model Validation: We execute comprehensive hyperparameter sweeps from K = 2 to K = 8, validating cluster defensibility through Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS), Silhouette coefficients, Davies-Bouldin indices, and 10-seed partition stability metrics (Adjusted Rand Index).

4) Dimensionality Reduction & Loading Analysis: We perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to decouple correlated pricing dimensions, verifying that 3 orthogonal components capture 88.6% of cumulative variance.

5) Yield Intelligence Operationalization: We operationalize the discovered clusters into an interactive consumer decision platform (AEROMATRIX / FarePredictor) that quantifies the 18–34 day "Goldilocks Booking Window" and computes financial savings relative to peak departure surge.

II. Theoretical Background & Related Work

A. Evolution of Airline Revenue Management

The mathematical foundations of revenue management were established by Littlewood [1] through two-fare inventory control rules, demonstrating that expected marginal revenue from full-fare passengers must equal or exceed discounted fares to justify reserving unallocated capacity. Belobaba [2] subsequently generalized this formulation into the Expected Marginal Seat Revenue (EMSR-a and EMSR-b) heuristics, enabling multi-nested booking control across multiple fare classes. Modern airline systems extend these principles through dynamic programming, network revenue management, and real-time bid-price controls that dynamically adjust fare availability based on network-wide shadow prices.

B. Machine Learning in Airfare Forecasting

In recent years, researchers have applied supervised machine learning to predict airline ticket price movements. Groves and Gini [8] designed automated software agents utilizing regression trees and linear models to optimize ticket purchase timing. Tziridis et al. [7] evaluated Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Random Forests to forecast airfares, demonstrating that ensemble methods achieve lower Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) than traditional time-series models. Similarly, Janssen [13] applied gradient-boosted decision trees to evaluate airline pricing dynamics across European low-cost carriers.

Despite their predictive utility, supervised models suffer from two major shortcomings in aviation economics: (a) they require explicit continuous target labels and cannot discover latent market segments; and (b) they suffer from high variance when generalized across diverse flight corridors with divergent baseline fare structures.

C. Unsupervised Clustering in Aviation Economics

Unsupervised learning has been utilized in aviation primarily for operational and spatial analysis. Lacoste et al. [11] applied spectral clustering to classify air traffic flows and airspace sector congestion. Other studies have clustered airports based on hub centrality, passenger throughput, or runway delay propagation [14]. However, limited academic literature has addressed the problem of segmenting flight routes specifically by their dynamic pricing elasticity and advance booking curves.

Furthermore, standard machine learning toolkits overwhelmingly default to Euclidean K-Means clustering. In econometric datasets characterized by asymmetric price spikes, Euclidean distance squares errors, magnifying outlier leverage. This paper addresses this gap by establishing a formal benchmark between Euclidean (L2) and Manhattan (L1) distance metrics for airfare market segmentation.

III. Dataset Architecture & Preprocessing

A. Data Source and Ingestion

The empirical research data is derived from the Kaggle Flight Price Prediction dataset (Clean_Dataset.csv) [12], augmented and expanded to capture 70,000+ flight records across 112 commercial corridors. The dataset documents commercial flight offerings across primary Indian metropolitan hubs (Delhi, Mumbai, Bangalore, Kolkata, Hyderabad, Chennai), regional holiday centers (Goa, Pune, Ahmedabad, Kochi, Jaipur, Srinagar), and global intercontinental gateways (London Heathrow, Dubai International, Singapore Changi, New York JFK, Paris Charles de Gaulle, Bangkok Suvarnabhumi, Tokyo Narita, Frankfurt).

B. Feature Schema

The raw transaction schema records ticket-level variables including: (i) operating carrier; (ii) flight code; (iii) origin city; (iv) departure time slot; (v) number of intermediate stops; (vi) destination city; (vii) arrival time slot; (viii) cabin class (Economy vs. Business); (ix) flight duration in hours; (x) days remaining until flight departure (booking lead time, d ∈ [1, 49]); and (xi) transaction ticket price in Indian Rupees (INR).

C. Missing Value Imputation and Data Integrity

1) Median Imputation for Continuous Dimensions: Let X = {x1, x2, ..., xN} represent continuous flight prices. The arithmetic mean μ is known to have a breakdown point of 0%, meaning a single arbitrary outlier can distort the estimate arbitrarily. In contrast, the sample median:

Median(X) = x(⌊(N+1)/2⌋) (1)

possesses a breakdown point of 50%. Continuous variables (fares and durations) are therefore imputed using column medians, preventing distortion from emergency holiday fares exceeding ₹90,000.

2) Mode Imputation for Categorical Attributes: Categorical variables (departure slots, airlines) are imputed using the statistical mode (the most frequent category).

3) Integrity Validation: Observations where Origin equals Destination or where booking horizons exceed logical bounds are verified and purged.

D. Corridor-Level Aggregation

Because individual flight tickets reflect high-frequency transaction noise, we aggregate ticket records into discrete commercial corridors. Let T represent the set of all ticket observations. A commercial corridor Cij between origin i and destination j is formally defined as:

Cij = { t ∈ T | Origin(t) = i ∧ Destination(t) = j } (2)

This aggregation aggregates 70,000 flight observations into 112 commercial corridor vectors.

E. Z-Score Standardization

Clustering algorithms relying on distance calculations are sensitive to disparate feature scales. For instance, ticket prices span tens of thousands of INR, whereas direct flight ratios range between 0.0 and 1.0. To prevent monetary features from dominating duration and surge dimensions, all continuous features are transformed using Z-score standardization (StandardScaler):

zik = ( xik - μk ) / σk (3)

where xik is the raw value of feature k for corridor i, μk is the feature mean, and σk is the standard deviation. This transformation enforces zero mean and unit variance across all 9 dimensions.

To characterize the complex multi-dimensional pricing behavior of each corridor, we engineer 9 behavioral metrics grounded in aviation revenue management theory:

A. Coefficient of Variation (`price_cv`)

Absolute standard deviation σ is scale-dependent; an international route naturally exhibits larger nominal variance than a short domestic hop. The Coefficient of Variation normalizes standard deviation by the corridor mean fare:

CV = σ / μ (4)

This metric isolates genuine dynamic pricing volatility from absolute currency magnitude.

B. Last-Minute Surge Ratio (`surge_ratio`)

Airlines exploit passenger urgency in the final days before flight departure. We define the surge ratio as the ratio of average fares booked within 3 days of departure to fares booked at least 21 days in advance:

Surge = P̄(d ≤ 3) / P̄(d ≥ 21) (5)

Corridors with surge ratios exceeding 2.0x reflect aggressive yield management and low passenger price elasticity.

C. Dynamic Price Spread (`price_spread`)

To eliminate extreme statistical noise at the outer tails, price spread is formulated as the difference between the 90th percentile fare (P90) and the 10th percentile fare (P10):

Spread = P90 - P10 (6)

This captures the complete operational pricing envelope utilized by carrier revenue managers.

V. Clustering Methodology & Distance Benchmarks

A. K-Means Optimization Formulation

Let X = {x1, x2, ..., xn} be the set of n = 112 standardized corridor feature vectors in ℝ9. The K-Means algorithm partitions X into K disjoint subsets S = {S1, S2, ..., SK} by solving the optimization problem:

arg minS Σk=1..K Σx∈Sk ||x - μk||22 (7)

where μk is the centroid of cluster Sk:

μk = (1 / |Sk|) Σx∈Sk x (8)

B. Centroid Seeding via k-means++

Standard random initialization is vulnerable to suboptimal local minima. We employ k-means++ [3], which initializes centroids sequentially. The first center μ1 is selected uniformly at random. Each subsequent center μk is chosen from the remaining observations with probability:

P(x) = D(x)2 / Σx'∈X D(x')2 (9)

where D(x) denotes the shortest Euclidean distance from point x to the closest centroid already chosen. This guarantees an expected approximation factor within O(log K) of the global optimum.

C. Distance Metric Formulation

We benchmark two geometric distance formulations across the 9-dimensional space:

1) Euclidean Distance (L2 Norm):

d2(x, y) = ( Σj=1..9 (xj - yj)2 )1/2 (10)

2) Manhattan Distance (L1 Norm / Taxicab):

d1(x, y) = Σj=1..9 |xj - yj| (11)

D. Analytical Proof: Manhattan Robustness on Skewed Fares

Consider a cluster Sk containing observations along a price dimension x. Under the Euclidean objective, the loss function minimizes sum-of-squares errors: L2(c) = Σ (xi - c)2. Setting the first derivative to zero yields:

∂L2 / ∂c = -2 Σ (xi - c) = 0 ⟹ c = (1/N) Σ xi = μ (12)

Because error is squared, an extreme pricing outlier xoutlier ≫ μ exerts quadratic force, shifting c toward the outlier.

Under Manhattan distance, the loss function minimizes absolute deviations: L1(c) = Σ |xi - c|. Differentiating with respect to c:

∂L1 / ∂c = - Σ sgn(xi - c) = 0 (13)

Equation (13) is satisfied if and only if the number of observations strictly greater than c equals the number strictly less than c. Thus, the optimal center c under Manhattan distance is the median:

c* = Median({xi}) (14)

Since the median is invariant to the absolute magnitude of tail observations, Manhattan clustering produces compact clusters anchored in genuine route pricing norms.

VI. Experimental Results & Quantitative Evaluation

A. Multi-K Hyperparameter Sweeps

To determine the mathematically defensible number of route archetypes, we conducted hyperparameter sweeps from K = 2 through K = 8. Three quantitative criteria were evaluated:

1) Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS / Inertia): Measures cluster compactness.

2) Silhouette Coefficient (s) [4]: Measures relative cohesion versus separation. For point i:

s(i) = ( b(i) - a(i) ) / max( a(i), b(i) ) (15)

where a(i) is mean intra-cluster distance, and b(i) is mean distance to the nearest neighboring cluster. The overall score is the mean over all N samples.

3) Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI) [5]: Evaluates similarity between each cluster and its most similar counterpart:

DBI = (1/K) Σi=1..K maxj≠i ( (si + sj) / d(μi, μj) ) (16)

where si is average distance of all points in cluster i to centroid μi. Lower DBI values indicate superior separation.

B. Empirical Justification for K = 4

As documented in Table II, the WCSS inertia curve demonstrates a distinct elbow bend at K = 4, dropping steeply from 459.54 (K = 2) to 241.66 (K = 4). Beyond K = 4, marginal reductions in inertia diminish sharply (192.20 at K = 5, 151.97 at K = 6).

Crucially, under the Manhattan (L1) metric, the Silhouette coefficient achieves 0.4858 at K = 4, representing an 11.2% improvement over the standard Euclidean formulation (0.4369). This quantitative evidence confirms that absolute deviation medians yield tighter intra-cluster cohesion and cleaner boundary separation.

C. Partition Stability Analysis

To establish that the discovered clusters are not artifacts of centroid initialization randomness, we executed 10 independent clustering iterations across randomized seeds (`random_state` ∈ [0, 1000]). Partition agreement was measured using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) [6] and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI):

ARI = ( Index - Expected ) / ( Max - Expected ) (17)

The stability experiments yielded: (a) Mean ARI = 0.9927 ± 0.0078; (b) Mean NMI = 0.9871 ± 0.0137. An Adjusted Rand Index of 0.9927 confirms 99.3% deterministic stability across randomized trials, proving that the corridor clusters reflect genuine structural properties.

VII. Dimensionality Reduction via PCA

To eliminate feature collinearity and visualize the 9-dimensional space, we performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [9]. PCA computes the orthogonal eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix Σ = (1/n) XTX, solving the characteristic equation:

Σ v = λ v (18)

where v represents the eigenvectors (principal axes) and λ represents the corresponding eigenvalues (variance explained).

IX. Operationalization: The FarePredictor Engine

To demonstrate practical utility, the mathematical cluster parameters were deployed into a full-stack web application (FarePredictor) providing real-time booking guidance.

A. Percentile Spectrum Formulation

When a traveler queries an origin and destination for a departure date d days ahead, the engine computes the current fare's percentile rank PR relative to historical benchmarks:

PR = min( 95, max( 5, ( (P - P10) / (P90 - P10) ) × 100 ) ) (19)

B. The "Goldilocks Booking Window"

Analysis of historical yield curves demonstrates that airline dynamic pricing follows a distinct tri-phase trajectory:

1) Premature Window (d > 35 days): Airlines hold baseline seat allocations at moderate-to-high standard tariff rates. If current fare exceeds the 50th percentile, the system advises "⏳ WAIT".

2) The Goldilocks Window (18 ≤ d ≤ 34 days): Carriers release unallocated capacity into discounted inventory buckets. Fares reach their lowest stable historical equilibrium (25th percentile). The platform triggers a "⚡ BOOK NOW" verdict.

3) Surge Danger Zone (d ≤ 7 days): In the final week, dynamic pricing escalates aggressively as business passengers purchase remnant seats. The platform issues an urgent "⚡ BOOK NOW" recommendation to avoid price penalties.

C. Financial Savings Computation

The system quantifies the exact monetary savings achieved by locking in fares today versus peak departure surge:

Savings = max( 0, Ppeak_surge - Pcurrent ) (20)

On long-haul routes such as DEL ➔ LON, booking within the Goldilocks window yields savings of up to ₹38,000 (44%).

X. Sensitivity & Ablation Studies

To evaluate the impact of preprocessing choices, we conducted ablation studies across feature subsets and scaling strategies:

1) Scaling Strategy Comparison: We evaluated StandardScaler against MinMaxScaler and RobustScaler. StandardScaler produced the highest Silhouette cohesion (0.4858) by preserving relative variance ratios, whereas MinMaxScaler compressed feature variance due to extreme international price ranges.

2) Feature Importance Ablation: Removing price_cv or surge_ratio reduced Silhouette scores from 0.4858 to 0.4120, confirming that dynamic volatility dimensions are critical for separating domestic trunks from regional shuttles.

XI. Threats to Validity & Limitations

1) Observation Window Truncation: The Kaggle dataset records flight bookings up to 49 days prior to departure, omitting ultra-early bird inventory released 6 to 12 months ahead.

2) Survivorship Bias: Web-scraped pricing captures available seats. Flights that sell out completely vanish from scrapers, biasing late-stage samples toward premium remnant seats.

3) Geographic Concentration: The dataset focuses primarily on Tier-1 Indian hubs, limiting immediate generalization to secondary regional Tier-3 airstrips.

4) Exogenous Factor Omission: Macroeconomic shocks, such as jet fuel price shifts, weather disruptions, and airline labor negotiations, are unobserved in ticket scrape data.

XII. Ethical Considerations & Consumer Protection

Airline dynamic pricing algorithms exploit consumer vulnerability during non-discretionary emergency travel (e.g. medical treatments, bereavement). By providing transparent historical percentile rankings and probabilistic booking recommendations, this research reduces information asymmetry, empowering consumers against predatory surge pricing.

XIII. Conclusion & Future Work

This study demonstrates that unsupervised machine learning successfully segments commercial flight corridors according to historical pricing behavior. By engineering 9 behavioral dimensions across 112 corridors from 70,000+ flight records, we established that Manhattan distance ($L_1$) clustering outperforms Euclidean distance ($L_2$) by +11.2% in Silhouette cohesion (0.4858 vs 0.4369), providing outlier resilience on skewed financial distributions. Multi-K sweeps confirmed $K$ = 4 as the defensible structural partition, supported by a WCSS elbow at 241.66 and 99.3% partition stability across 10 random seeds. The discovered archetypes were operationalized into a real-time decision engine that isolates the 18–34 day Goldilocks window to unlock consumer savings of up to 44%.

Future research will integrate airline load factors, jet fuel price indices, and deep reinforcement learning to dynamically optimize dual-sided carrier and passenger booking strategies.
